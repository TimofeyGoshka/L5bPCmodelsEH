Cellular mechanisms of serotonergic psychedelics - apical hypercontextualisation




Abstract


Classical serotonergic psychedelics primarily exert their profound effects through agonism at the serotonin 2A (5-HT2A) receptor, which is abundantly expressed in many cortical regions, particularly in layer V pyramidal neurons of associative and visual areas. At the cellular level, these receptors are predominantly localized postsynaptically on the soma and apical dendrites. This review synthesizes evidence from distinct subfields of psychedelic cognitive neuroscience to illuminate how 5-HT2A agonism at apical dendrites of layer V pyramidal neurons disrupts the usual boundaries of conscious mental representations, boosting their interaction with surrounding contextual influences. We propose "apical hypercontextualization" as a unifying mechanism, whereby enhanced apical signaling amplifies relational processing over direct stimulus representation. Finally, we overview key psychedelic phenomenological features - such as perceptual distortions, associative cognition, and self-alterations - demonstrating how they emerge from contextual amplification, with implications for therapeutic mechanisms in mental health disorders.

1. Introduction


Serotonergic psychedelics are a broad class of compounds which profoundly alter cognitive, affective and perceptual processing. Iconic examples include psilocybin/psilocin (main psychoactive component of magic mushrooms), lysergic acid diethylamide (LSD), N,N-dimethyltryptamine (DMT), 5-methoxy-N,N-dimethyltryptamine (5-MeO-DMT) and mescaline, as well as synthesized compounds mimicking the structure of serotonin. Among many others the latter include more commonly investigated substances such as 2,5-dimethoxy-4-iodoamphetamine (DOI), 2,5-dimethoxy-4-bromoamphetamine (DOB) and (7R)-3-bromo-2,5-dimethoxy-bicyclo[4.2.0]octa-1,3,5-trien-7-yl]methanamine (TCB-2). 


Although largely forgotten for over three decades and still strictly scheduled in most parts of the world, psychedelics are again subject to serious scientific inquiry. Renewed interest has been largely due to their transdiagnostic therapeutic potential with preliminary research finding beneficial effects in treating depression (Agin-Liebes et al., 2020; Carhart-Harris, Bolstridge, et al., 2016; Carhart-Harris et al., 2021; Davis, Barrett, May, et al., 2021; Raison et al., 2023; Rosenblat et al., 2024; von Rotz et al., 2023), anxiety (Danforth et al., 2018; Dos Santos et al., 2021; Holze et al., 2023, 2024), substance use (Bogenschutz et al., 2015, 2022; Garcia-Romeu et al., 2014, 2019, 2020; Johnson et al., 2014, 2017) and migraines (Schindler et al., 2021, 2022), to name a few. This broad set of ailments for which psychedelics can provide relief is underlined by a wide range of proposed pharmacological, neural, cognitive or psychological therapeutic mechanisms (van Elk & Yaden, 2022), however a comprehensive understanding has remained elusive. While scientific focus has mainly been on therapeutic use and mechanisms, research into the basic mechanisms of action (especially cognitive and psychological) have been somewhat neglected. Understanding the fundamental mechanisms of psychedelic action is crucially important to clarify their therapeutic and consciousness-altering effects and can furthermore help figure out the mechanisms of various mental disorders, consciousness and perceptual phenomena.


Although it is commonly asserted that the effect of serotonergic psychedelics is directed at the apical (or ‘distal’) dendrites of layer V pyramidal neurons (e.g., Nichols, 2016; Vollenweider & Preller, 2020), this anatomical fact prevailingly remains an insubstantial digression within the discussion on mechanisms of psychedelic action. We address this oversight by providing a concise and detailed overview of the relation between apical dendrites, 5-HT2A receptor and psychedelic action. Then, we build on the overview to suggest a framework of psychedelic action on the cellular level and highlight broad evidence how the targeted effect of psychedelics on the apical compartment suggests contextual modulation as a central mechanism of serotonergic psychedelics.




2. Review of foundations


1. 5-HT2A receptor - the main player in psychedelic action


All serotonergic psychedelics bind to a broad range of different neurotransmitter receptors (Jain et al., 2025; Ray, 2010; Rickli et al., 2016). However, serotonin (5-hydroxytryptamine, 5-HT) receptor subtype 2A (the 5-HT2A receptor) seems to be key for psychedelic effects (Hatzipantelis & Olson, 2024; McClure-Begley & Roth, 2022; Nichols, 2016). The 5-HT2A receptor is one of 14 different serotonin receptor subtypes found within most mammals. Similarly to most serotonin receptors (with the exception of 5-HT3) it is a metabotropic (i.e., G-protein coupled) receptor. The 5-HT2A receptor is canonically thought to activate the Gq- pathway leading to intracellular calcium (Ca2+) signalling and protein kinase C (PKC) activity. However, some findings suggest that 5-HT2A agonism can also initiate Gs and Gi signaling (Liu et al., 2022). Moreover, research indicates that the Gq pathway could be only partially responsible for psychedelic effects (Garcia et al., 2007), and that 5-HT2A-dependent Gs (Liu et al., 2022), Gi (González-Maeso et al., 2007; Kossatz et al., 2024) or β-arrestin-2 (Rodriguiz et al., 2021) activity could also bear at least some of the weight. 


There is a near consensus in the field that the bulk of psychedelic effects depends on effects at the 5-HT2A receptor (Lambe & Aghajanian, 2006a). Several avenues of research provide evidence for this view. Treatment with 5-HT2A receptor antagonists has been found to block (Holze, Caluori, et al., 2021; Kometer et al., 2012, 2013; Kraehenmann, Pokorny, Aicher, et al., 2017; Preller et al., 2017, 2018; Quednow et al., 2012; Vollenweider et al., 1998) as well as reverse (Becker et al., 2023) the effects of psychedelics. The intensity of the subjective effects of psychedelics has been found to correlate with 5-HT2A receptor occupancy in positron emission tomography studies (Madsen et al., 2019; Stenbæk et al., 2021). 5-HT2A receptor knockout has also been found to abolish the head-twitch response (HTR, a common behavioural assay for psychedelic effects; Halberstadt et al., 2020) in rodents (González-Maeso et al., 2007; Halberstadt et al., 2011). Several computational modeling efforts also indicate that 5-HT2A receptor distribution best explains psychedelic effects on whole-brain activity (Deco et al., 2018; Herzog et al., 2023; Kringelbach et al., 2020; Singleton et al., 2025). 


Moreover, blocking the binding of psychedelic compounds to other receptors does not seem to block the subjective effects. For example, 5-HT2C-receptor antagonists have been found to enhance psychedelic effects at lower doses (Erkizia-Santamaría et al., 2022; Shahar et al., 2022), but reduce them at higher doses (Shahar et al., 2022). Blocking the 5-HT1A-receptor does not abolish the main psychedelic effects (Canal, 2018; Erkizia-Santamaría et al., 2022; Fox et al., 2010), however it might enhance (Strassman, 1995) or modulate (e.g., Pokorny et al., 2016; Shahar et al., 2022) the acute effects and could account for some specific effects (e.g., binocular rivalry, obsessive behaviour; Carter et al., 2007; Halberstadt et al., 2011; Singh et al., 2023). The non-selective 5-HT2A/2C antagonist ketanserin does not block some affective effects of psychedelics (especially relating to processing of positively-valenced stimuli), suggesting that they might be mediated by other receptors (Holze, Avedisian, et al., 2021; Kometer et al., 2012). Furthermore, non-psychedelic alterations of consciousness are also linked with the 5-HT2A receptor as highlighted by the use of 5-HT2A (Sommer et al., 2018) and combined 5-HT2A/D2 antagonists (Meltzer, 2013) in treatment of positive psychotic symptoms and increased 5-HT2A density in patients with visual hallucinations (Ballanger et al., 2010; Huot et al., 2010).


2. Localization of the 5-HT2A receptor


The 5-HT2A receptor, responsible for the majority of psychedelic-induced alterations in consciousness, is expressed widely across the brain (Fig 1C). It is particularly abundant in many cortical regions, although it is also present in low to moderate density in subcortical areas (Beliveau et al., 2017; Varnäs et al., 2004). In the human brain, high concentrations of 5-HT2A receptors are typically observed in associative and transmodal regions, with the visual cortex standing out as the only unimodal area exhibiting similarly elevated levels (Beliveau et al., 2017; Luppi et al., 2024; Varnäs et al., 2004).


The specific cortical laminar organization of 5-HT2A receptors, particularly in primates and humans, remains less understood and warrants further research (however, see Mengod et al., 2015). Existing evidence suggests that 5-HT2A receptors can be found in most cortical layers (Jensen et al., 2020) dependent on specific regions (Varnäs et al., 2004). However, modern studies with rodents reveal a pronounced presence of 5-HT2A receptors on the soma of layer Va pyramidal neurons and their apical dendrites infiltrating layer I (Fig 1B; Chiu et al., 2023; Jensen et al., 2020; Weber, 2010; see also Xu & Pandey, 2000). Some evidence suggests that in primates, this laminar distribution may be less sharply defined (De Almeida & Mengod, 2007; Mengod et al., 2015; Savalia et al., 2021; Watakabe et al., 2009), although this could reflect limitations in techniques such as autoradiography and in situ mRNA hybridization. To resolve these uncertainties, the use of more precise tools — such as highly specific antibodies, advanced tracers, or signal amplification methods — is essential for accurately mapping 5-HT2A receptor distribution in primates and humans. 


5-HT2A receptors are mainly expressed in pyramidal cells, but also in a subset of GABAergic interneurons (e.g., calbindin, somatostatin and fast-spiking parvalbumin interneurons; Andrade, 2011; Bombardi, 2018; De Filippo et al., 2021; Jakab & Goldman-Rakic, 1998, 2000; Ju et al., 2020; Mengod et al., 2015; Weber, 2010; Willins et al., 1997). In the human prefrontal cortex 5-HT2A receptor mRNA has been reported to be found in 95 - 98% of layer II-V pyramidal cells and in around 75% of layer VI pyramidal cells (De Almeida & Mengod, 2007). 


5-HT2A receptors are localized on cytoplasmic membranes (Cornea-Hebert et al., 1999; Doherty & Pickel, 2000; Vargas et al., 2023) as well as on the outer plasma membrane (Willins et al., 1997). In the cytoplasm, they are likely to be localized to the membranes of certain intracellular organelles such as the smooth endoplasmic reticulum and its related vesicles (Doherty & Pickel, 2000; Willins et al., 1997) or the Golgi complex (Vargas et al., 2023). Evidence suggests that the bulk of 5-HT2A receptors are localized intracellularly within the soma (Chiu et al., 2023; Doherty & Pickel, 2000; Vargas et al., 2023; Weber & Andrade, 2010). However, the proportion of functionally active intracellular 5-HT2A receptors and the precise nature of their function is yet unclear.


Outer plasma membrane 5-HT2A receptors are mainly found in dendritic regions and seldom on axonal sections (Miner et al., 2003; Weber & Andrade, 2010; T. Xu & Pandey, 2000). In the rare cases where 5-HT2A receptors are expressed axonally, they are on monoaminergic neurons (Doherty & Pickel, 2000; Marek, 2017; Miner et al., 2003). On pyramidal neurons, 5-HT2A receptors are primarily located on apical dendrites (Chiu et al., 2023; Jakab & Goldman-Rakic, 1998; Miner et al., 2003; Willins et al., 1997; T. Xu & Pandey, 2000). While less specifically investigated, studies suggest that 5-HT2A receptors could also be found on basal and oblique dendrites (Chiu et al., 2023; Jakab & Goldman-Rakic, 1998; Willins et al., 1997; however, see Weber & Andrade, 2010). However, their localization at the basal dendrites has been difficult to confirm, as it could be hard to distinguish their signal from somatic receptors. There is also evidence suggesting that 5-HT2A receptors could be located on heads and necks of dendritic spines (Jakab & Goldman-Rakic, 1998; Miner et al., 2003). While earlier studies suggested that apical 5-HT2A receptors are mostly located on the proximal part of the dendrite (Jakab & Goldman-Rakic, 1998), later and more specific studies show the 5-HT2A receptor also located in the distal part of apical dendrites (Cornea-Hébert et al., 2002; Weber & Andrade, 2010). There is also evidence of 5-HT2A receptors on the apical tuft regions (highly arborized distal sections protruding from the main dendritic trunk) in layer I (Chiu et al., 2023). 


Overall, the 5-HT2A receptor is most abundant on layer V excitatory pyramidal cells and their dendrites, with less frequent localization on pyramidal cells within other laminae and GABAergic interneurons. The bulk of 5-HT2A receptors are mainly localized intracellularly within the soma. Regarding dendritic localization, 5-HT2A receptors preferably sit within postsynaptic regions with outer plasma membrane receptors (i.e., receptors most likely responsible for intercellular communication) clearly found in the apical compartment and likely also on basal dendrites.


3. Layer V pyramidal neurons


Neocortical layer V, the primary laminar target of psychedelics, is predominantly composed of pyramidal neurons with some of the largest cortical dendritic branches extending into layer I. The complex dendritic arborization of layer V pyramidal neurons renders them the key integrative elements within the neocortex (Aru, Suzuki, et al., 2020; Ramaswamy & Markram, 2015). Such breadth of synaptic connectivity also underlies their second crucial function as the main output stream from the cortex connecting to many subcortical structures, including prominent projections to the thalamus (Harris & Shepherd, 2015; Jones, 2001; Llinás et al., 1998; Suzuki et al., 2023). This anatomical connectivity underpins the crucial role of these neurons in several general cognitive processes, such as conscious sensory perception and contextual modulation (Aru, Suzuki, et al., 2020; Ranganathan et al., 2018; Suzuki & Larkum, 2020; Takahashi et al., 2016, 2020).
Layer V pyramidal neurons are commonly characterized by having three distinct dendritic compartments (fig 1A): a ramulose apical tuft compartment, which branches out from an apical trunk into layer I; sparse oblique dendrites in the middle and proximal parts of the apical trunk protruding at various angles; and a basal compartment with dendrites extending laterally from the soma (Aru, Suzuki, et al., 2020; Ramaswamy & Markram, 2015). Oblique dendrites receive cortico-cortical input from nearby pyramidal- and interneurons in layer 2-4, as well as thalamocortical input (Rah et al., 2013). Basal dendrites receive intralaminar (primarily local) cortical input and low-order thalamic input (Bannister, 2005; Constantinople & Bruno, 2013).  


4. Contextual function of the apical compartment of layer V pyramidal neurons
 
 
Layer V pyramidal neurons have long been touted as important for consciousness due to their anatomical and connectomic properties (Crick & Koch, 1992; Ramon y Cajal, 1894). The effects of psychedelics further corroborate the central role of these cells in conscious processing. After all, the key target of psychedelics - the 5-HT2A receptor - is primarily localized within layer V pyramidal neurons and their apical dendrites (Chiu et al., 2023; Weber & Andrade, 2010). Recently, it has been proposed that consciousness depends on coupling between the apical and basal compartments within these cells (Aru, Suzuki, et al., 2020). This idea is based on the evidence that anaesthetic compounds decouple the basal and apical dendritic compartments (Suzuki & Larkum, 2020). Furthermore, optogenetic activation of the apical compartment of layer V pyramidal cells has been found to induce learned licking behaviour in rodents as if whisker stimulus was present (Takahashi et al., 2016), which has been speculated to indicate hallucinatory perception (Aru, Siclari, et al., 2020; Phillips et al., 2025) and suggests an important functional role for this dendritic compartment for contents of consciousness.
 
Multiple avenues of scientific inquiry suggest that the apical compartment is responsible for contextual processing. Anatomical and functional connectivity studies highlight how the input into the apical compartment is contextual and associative in nature (as opposed to feature or object-based; Muckli et al., 2015; Roth et al., 2016; for reviews see Huang et al., 2024; Phillips, 2023). Apical dendrites of layer V pyramidal neurons gather input from within a cortical column as well as from distant cortical (Cauller et al., 1998) and subcortical (Roth et al., 2016; Rubio-Garrido et al., 2009) regions. In contrast, input running into the basal compartment is largely from nearby intra- and interlaminar cells (Bannister, 2005). This mixture of both global and local afferents incident on the apical compartment has been suggested to provide context for ongoing processing (Aru, Suzuki, et al., 2020; Bachmann et al., 2020; Larkum, 2013; Phillips, 2023; Phillips et al., 2018; Spratling, 2002). Furthermore, the apical compartment, via receiving both nearby and distant contextual information (Phillips et al., 2018), could also instantiate the cellular substrate for the boundaries between mental objects (Aghajanian, 2009; Bayne & Carter, 2018), thereby also being crucially necessary for their representational definition. 
 
More specific studies investigating apical signalling also provide strong indication of its contextual role. Calcium signals within the apical dendrite have been found to be linked with behavioural context (essentially environmental setting; Takahashi et al., 2020). Conditioned response learning also leads to plasticity effects in the apical compartment, but not in the basal compartment (Godenzini et al., 2022; see also Benezra et al., 2024). Integration of sensory and motor information also depends on processing within the apical compartment (N. Xu et al., 2012). Several computational models investigating the information processing of the brain demonstrate the computational benefits of having a separate apical dendritic compartment with contextual information (Baronig & Legenstein, 2024; Kay & Phillips, 2011; Körding & König, 2000; Schmid & Neumann, 2025; see also Guerguiev et al., 2017) .


Thalamic connectivity to and from layer V pyramidal neurons is also likely relevant for the apical contextual function (Aru et al., 2019; Aru, Suzuki, et al., 2020; Bachmann et al., 2020; Larkum, 2013). Thalamus not only provides primary sensory information, but rather also modulates cortical activity through higher-order feedback and is linked with conscious processing.  Several lines of research support the role of the thalamus in contextual processing. Many studies directly indicate that contextual processing relies on thalamic activity (e.g., Chen et al., 2024; Law & Smith, 2012; Marchand et al., 2014; Peelman & Haider, 2025; Roth et al., 2016; see also Bast et al., 2025). Furthermore, higher-order thalamus controls cortical networks (Chen et al., 2024; Halassa & Kastner, 2017; Roth et al., 2016; Russo et al., 2025; Shine et al., 2023; Suzuki et al., 2023) and several computational models also support that thalamic processing supports contextual processes within the cortex (Schmitt et al., 2017; Zheng et al., 2024). The connectivity between higher-order thalamic nuclei and the cortex also seems to underlie associative processes. Several higher-order and diffuse thalamic regions receive ‘driver’ inputs from layer V pyramidal cells (Sherman & Guillery, 1996, 1998), in turn providing contextual information to other cortical areas. For example, lesioning either the mediodorsal thalamus or medial prefrontal cortex both disrupt associative recognition and recency but not item recognition (Cross et al., 2013). 




5. The neural and dendritic effects of psychedelics  


Canonically, the direct neural effect of psychedelics is considered to be excitatory, depending predominantly on the 5-HT2A receptor which primarily induces an excitatory effect on neural firing upon conjoining with its endogenous ligand, serotonin (Araneda & Andrade, 1991). A host of studies, both old and emerging, indicate that the total additive effects of psychedelics lead to mainly decreased brain activity (reviewed in Hidalgo Jiménez et al., 2025; see also section V.B). Considerable evidence also indicates that some inhibitory neural effects are directly 5-HT2A-mediated (Ashby et al., 1990; Bambico et al., 2010; El Mansari & Blier, 2005; Puig, 2003; Tian et al., 2016; Wang et al., 2025; see also Carr et al., 2002). However, recent studies that have preferentially looked at layer V neurons with higher 5-HT2A levels have found a tendency towards excitatory activity (Muir et al., 2024; Schmitz et al., 2025). This has been shown both using electrophysiology (Béïque et al., 2007; Puig, 2003; Schmitz et al., 2025) and calcium-imaging (Muir et al., 2024; Shao et al., 2025). The clearest excitatory response to psychedelics seems to be found in extratelencephalic layer V neurons (Shao et al., 2025; see also Celada et al., 2008; Hidalgo Jiménez et al., 2025; Puig, 2003; Riga et al., 2014). Altogether, psychedelics seem to have bidirectional effects on individual neurons. A few studies have also reported this directly (Michaiel et al., 2019; Muir et al., 2024; Watakabe et al., 2009). Early evidence suggests that the directionality of the effect could depend on specific cell type (e.g., Martin & Nichols, 2016; Shao et al., 2025) or initial firing rate (Dearnley, 2023; Michaiel et al., 2019; Watakabe et al., 2009).


Psychedelics also seem to have distinct effects at different dendritic compartments. Several lines of evidence support that psychedelics enhance apical activity through both pre- and postsynaptic mechanisms. Existing electrophysiological results of psychedelic action have actually found clearer results in terms of presynaptic effects (Hidalgo Jiménez et al., 2025; Marek & Schoepp, 2021), which is surprising as the major portion of 5-HT2A receptors is postsynaptic. One of the earliest findings that distinguished psychedelics was an apical late component to evoked postsynaptic currents (Aghajanian & Marek, 1999a). This was later understood to be a mixture of both excitatory and inhibitory currents arising from recurrent network activity (Aghajanian, 2009; Marek, 2017). This late component was suggested to underlie a network UP state, increasing the susceptibility to other internal and external stimuli (Aghajanian, 2009; Lambe & Aghajanian, 2006b, 2007). As these recurrent effects are potently blocked by mGluR2/3 and adenosine A1 agonists, they are thought to be caused by increased glutamate levels - another distinct effect of psychedelic activity (Lambe & Aghajanian, 2006b; Muschamp et al., 2004; Scruggs et al., 2003; Stutzmann et al., 2001). This effect is also likely to prefer the apical compartment in layer I, where many afferents terminate (Marek, 2017).


Moreover, psychedelics have been found to increase the frequency of sEPSCs/sEPSPs (Aghajanian & Marek, 1997; Barre et al., 2016; Pałucha-Poniewiera et al., 2008; Wang et al., 2025), which is likely due to recurrent network activity (Marek, 2017) or presynaptic thalamocortical 5-HT2A agonism (Barre et al., 2016). However, it is important to note that psychedelics, as partial agonists at the 5-HT2A receptor, have considerably weaker (estimated ~10%) effects on sEPSC frequency (Aghajanian & Marek, 1997, 1999b; Lambe & Aghajanian, 2006a). This casts doubt on the effect being primarily relevant for the acute psychedelic effects. Various psychedelics have also been found to increase the amplitude of evoked EPSCs/EPSPs in the apical compartment (Aghajanian & Marek, 1997; Zhai et al., 2003), but not spontaneous EPSCs/EPSPs (Schmitz et al., 2025; Wang et al., 2025). Because of this and the dependence of psychedelic-evoked EPSCs on glutamatergic signaling, the effect of psychedelics on apical dendritic currents is likely to constitute a modulation of intrinsic excitatory transmission. As the evoked EPSC amplitude is decreased by serotonin (Aghajanian & Marek, 1997) it is likely that increased evEPSC amplitude is characteristic of psychedelics. Emerging evidence is also suggesting that psychedelics increase Ca2+ signaling within apical dendrites of projecting pyramidal tract (extratelencephalic; ET) neurons (Shao et al., 2025).
 
Serotonergic psychedelics also inhibit EPSCs triggered by bath application of NMDA and lead to AMPA receptor internalization within the layer V pyramidal neurons (Arvanov, Liang, Magro, et al., 1999; Berthoux et al., 2019). However, when stimulating L1, a sustained increase in NMDA-mediated currents has been reported (Barre et al., 2016). A less specific application of NMDA has been found to lead to decreased somatic NMDA currents (Arvanov, Liang, Magro, et al., 1999; Arvanov, Liang, Russo, et al., 1999) potentially indicating that the basal dendrites could be inhibited by psychedelics (Hidalgo Jiménez et al., 2025). Relatedly, focal microiontophoretic application of serotonin into the apical compartment induced EPSCs (Aghajanian & Marek, 1997). This effect was not observed after injection into the basal compartment, which also suggests that apical activity is the key process targeted by psychedelics.


While the majority of 5-HT2A receptors are postsynaptic, a smaller, but considerable portion can also be found presynaptically on thalamocortical afferents originating from midline and intralaminar thalamic nuclei (Barre et al., 2016; Bécamel et al., 2017; Marek & Schoepp, 2021). Psychedelics modulate brain activity and psychedelic cognitive alterations via presynaptic activity by increasing apical glutamate levels and EPSCs (Barre et al., 2016; Bécamel et al., 2017), however their characteristic effects seem to also depend on postsynaptic apical activity within cortical layer V pyramidal neurons (González-Maeso et al., 2007).


In summary, psychedelics have complex effects on neuronal activity. While psychedelic 5-HT2A-agonism has bidirectional effects on neuronal activity, extratelencephalic layer V cells, likely with high expression of 5-HT2A receptors, seem to be excited by psychedelics. The bidirectional effects of psychedelics depend on factors like cell type, baseline firing rate, and receptor expression, with both excitatory and inhibitory outcomes mediated by 5-HT2A receptor activity. Psychedelics also enhance apical dendritic activity on ET layer V pyramidal neurons through pre- and postsynaptic mechanisms, increasing glutamate levels and evoked excitatory potentials, while possibly inhibiting basal dendritic signaling.




       III. Apical hypercontextualisation


We propose apical hypercontextualisation as a framework of psychedelic action grounded in the anatomical and electrophysiological findings reviewed above. At its core, apical hypercontextualisation explicates cellular machinery leading to increased apical and altered thalamocortical loop activity through two main mechanisms comprising three primary components.


Mechanism 1: Psychedelics increase the sensitivity of the apical compartment in a subset of ET layer V pyramidal neurons. A subset of layer V pyramidal neurons, which seem to mainly be extratelencephalic and with higher 5-HT2A receptor levels, is mainly excited by psychedelic substances (González-Maeso et al., 2007; Martin & Nichols, 2016; Muir et al., 2024; Schmitz et al., 2025; Shao et al., 2025). Increased amplitude of evoked EPSCs/EPSPs (Aghajanian & Marek, 1997; Zhai et al., 2003) and calcium signaling within apical dendrites of ET layer V pyramidal neurons (Shao et al., 2025) suggest that this excitation could arise from the apical compartment. Increased amplitude of evEPSCs/evEPSPs (Aghajanian & Marek, 1997; Zhai et al., 2003) also points at postsynaptic amplification. Psychedelic cell-type dependent effects on dendritic and neural activity (Michaiel et al., 2019; Muir et al., 2024; Watakabe et al., 2009) and that their effects depend on cortical 5-HT2A receptors (González-Maeso et al., 2007) further suggest an important mechanistic role for 5-HT2A receptors on ET layer V pyramidal neurons. All in all, this evidence suggests a postsynaptic 5-HT2A receptor-dependent mechanism that amplifies apical signals in a subset of ET layer V pyramidal neurons.




Mechanism 2: Psychedelics increase thalamocortical glutamatergic signals through presynaptic 5-HT2A receptors. While the majority of 5-HT2A receptors are postsynaptic and on cortical neurons, there seems to be a functionally important subset of thalamic neurons with cortical projections that house 5-HT2A receptors. As highlighted above, presynaptically-driven 5-HT2A-receptor-dependent increase in apical glutamate levels seems to actually be one of the clearer electrophysiological signatures of psychedelics (Aghajanian, 2009; Hidalgo Jiménez et al., 2025; Lambe & Aghajanian, 2007; Marek, 2017; Marek & Schoepp, 2021). There is also evidence which suggests that presynaptic 5-HT2A receptors can have relevant functional consequences, being required for associative learning in mice (Barre et al., 2016). In essence, mechanism 2 consists of presynaptic 5-HT2A receptors amplifying glutamatergic signals within thalamocortical afferents to apical dendrites of ET layer V pyramidal neurons.


These mechanisms contain three crucial components vital in psychedelic activity: apical 5-HT2A receptors within layer V pyramidal neurons, presynaptic thalamocortical 5-HT2A receptors and glutamatergic activity (likely mainly mediated by NMDA GluN2B-subunit containing and mGluR2 receptors; fig 2). Both sets of 5-HT2A receptors have modulatory roles over excitatory glutamatergic signalling (Aghajanian, 2009; Marek, 2017). Psychedelics induce a late component in apical evoked EPSCs/EPSPs (Aghajanian & Marek, 1997, 1999b; Lambe & Aghajanian, 2006b) and enhance evoked apical EPSCs/EPSPs  (Aghajanian & Marek, 1997; Barre et al., 2016). However, they lack a similar effect on spontaneous EPSCs/EPSPs at physiologically relevant concentrations (Aghajanian & Marek, 1997, 1999b; Wang et al., 2025), which lends support to their modulatory nature. This can be further explained mechanistically by the mediation of presynaptic GluN2B-containing NMDA receptors, as these receptors need membrane depolarization (e.g., via presynaptic action potential arrival) in order to remove their Mg2+ block and be active, as we suggest in Hidalgo Jiménez et al., 2025). In other words, the psychedelic 5-HT2A effect is dependent on glutamatergic network activity (see also Davoudian et al., 2023). Mechanism 1 enhances the response to glutamatergic signals within apical dendrites of some subcortically-projecting layer V pyramidal neurons. Mechanism 2 boosts glutamate release in thalamocortical synapses incident on apical dendrites. 


The integration of all three components situates the effect of psychedelics within apical thalamocortical loops (fig 2). Psychedelics increase excitatory input from layer V pyramidal neurons into the thalamus (mechanism 1), leading to broadly distributed feedback into the apical dendrites, which is enhanced by mechanism 2. An important source of input into the layer I apical dendrites comes from thalamocortical afferents (Berendse & Groenewegen, 1991; S. Huang et al., 2024; Roth et al., 2016; Rubio-Garrido et al., 2009), which could help ‘close the loop’ and sustain reverberating thalamocortical loop activity. Such loop activity has been suggested to be vital for consciousness (Aru, Suzuki, et al., 2020). Bursting behaviour (brief sequences of high-frequency firing) in layer V pyramidal neurons is likely to mediate sustained loop activity (Aru, Suzuki, et al., 2020; Munn et al., 2023; Whyte et al., 2025). Some research has looked at how psychedelics influence bursting in layer V pyramidal neurons. Bursting behaviour has been thought to be underlied by coincidence of Ca2+-dependent apical signalling and backpropagating somatic action potentials. Also, both in vitro (Arvanov, Liang, Magro, et al., 1999; Spindle & Thomas, 2014) and in vivo studies (Celada et al., 2008; De Gregorio et al., 2021; Riga et al., 2014) have found psychedelics to alter bursting behaviour, likely enhancing bursting within cells with high 5-HT2A receptor expression (Celada et al., 2008). The changes seem to depend on cell type (e.g., distinguished by intrinsic excitability and 5-HT2A and other psychedelic-responsive receptor levels and efficacy; (Celada et al., 2008; Spindle & Thomas, 2014). However, there is some indication that psychedelics could decrease apical backpropagating action potentials (Carr et al., 2002), which have been proposed to underlie bursting behaviour through apical-somatic coupling (Larkum et al., 1999). However, as this study did not differentiate between different types of pyramidal cells, it might be that these results were not about the mentioned subset of ET layer V pyramidal neurons. Relevantly, a recent preprint has reported that DOI reverses the effects of isoflurane or propofol in rats (Huels et al., 2025), suggesting that psychedelics can increase dendritic coupling and reverse anesthesia-induced decoupling.


Reverberating activity in a thalamocortical loop can also induce a more strongly distributed signal via the thalamus (fig 3). The acute effects of psychedelics have been found to lead to broad alterations in thalamocortical connectivity reporting increases with many cortical regions (however, with some regional variability across studies; (Avram et al., 2022, 2024, 2025; Carhart-Harris et al., 2013; Delli Pizzi et al., 2023; Ghaw et al., 2024; Inserra, De Gregorio, Rezai, et al., 2021; Müller et al., 2017; Preller et al., 2018; Preller, Razi, et al., 2019; Tagliazucchi et al., 2016). While the precise role of different parts of the thalamus in psychedelic effects is yet to be fully elucidated, it seems likely that psychedelics can induce thalamocortical hyperconnectivity in both higher- and lower-order regions (Avram et al., 2022; Delli Pizzi et al., 2023). However, this effect could be dose-dependent, as higher-order thalamic regions seem to be more robustly affected at higher doses (Inserra et al., 2021; see also, Avram et al., 2022; Siegel et al., 2024) and lower doses might have more significant effects at primary regions (Delli Pizzi et al., 2023). Apical hypercontextualisation posits that the elementary effect of enhanced apical thalamocortical loops leads to increased global broadcasting of cortical information via (mainly higher-order) thalamic activity (Aru, Suzuki, et al., 2020).


As there are some conflicting findings regarding the postsynaptic effect of psychedelics on the apical compartment, we avoid narrowing on a specific mechanism for the effects of psychedelics within the apical compartment and propose multiple mechanistic possibilities. First, the psychedelic effect could be instantiated via their activity at the apical tuft. However, there are also suggestions that psychedelics decrease AMPA currents within tuft dendrites (Berthoux et al., 2019) and that psychedelic enhancement of postsynaptic NMDA-mediated EPSCs depends on facilitation of presynaptic NMDA receptors (Barre et al., 2016), hinting that the main postsynaptic effect could be closer to the soma. However, investigating this mechanism empirically is complicated by the multitude of tuft dendrites and potential variable effects across different dendritic branches. Second, the effect could rely upon increasing Ca2+ signals at the apical integration zone (Phillips et al., 2018) where the tuft dendrites converge at the trunk. Third, the effect could also rely upon affecting apical oblique dendrites modulating input from thalamic afferents and local (intracolumnar) lateral cortical input (e.g., Aru, Suzuki, et al., 2020). And fourth, somatic or dendritic intracellular 5-HT2A receptors (Vargas et al., 2023) could also enhance sensitivity to or imitate apical signals. It is most likely that all or some of these effects combine to bring about psychedelic effects. Moreover, psychedelics could also inhibit signals arriving at the basal compartment of layer V pyramidal neurons, further boosting the relative effect of the apical dendrites on somatic firing (Hidalgo Jiménez et al., 2025). 


This machinery brings us to the crucial general mechanism behind the acute action of serotonergic psychedelics: increased contextualisation of internal representations via their activity at the 5-HT2A receptor (see also Shine et al., 2022). Serotonergic psychedelics help sustain reverberating thalamocortical loops and distribute their content as context to other cortical areas. They also inflict the somatic activity of layer V pyramidal neurons to be primarily apically driven (Aru, Siclari, et al., 2020), as opposed to relying on apical-basal coupling (Aru, Suzuki, et al., 2020; Phillips et al., 2018). The basal compartment is mainly linked with local (intracolumnar) processing (Bannister, 2005) with apical tufts and oblique dendrites also integrating more distant projections (Cauller et al., 1998; Roth et al., 2016; Rubio-Garrido et al., 2009). Shift into more apical processing highlights the effect of psychedelics on shifting to a more relational and associative processing (intercolumnar, -cortical and corticocortical; Muckli et al., 2015; Roth et al., 2016; for a recent review see Huang et al., 2024) and away from direct object/stimulus/feature processing (intracolumnar; see also Aru, Suzuki, et al., 2020). An equivalent way of thinking would be that, psychedelics alter the boundaries between representations which has even been proposed as a general mechanism for psychedelic perceptual effects (Bayne & Carter, 2018; see also Aghajanian, 2009).




IV. Hypercontextualising psychedelics


Having laid the groundwork of how anatomy and electrophysiology points at psychedelics increasing contextual, relational and associative processing, we will now highlight findings from many subfields of psychedelic research that provide further support for the framework of apical hypercontextualisation.


1. Neuroimaging and high-frequency oscillations


Neuroimaging studies provide important clues that highlight increased associative communication. Shifts into a more global processing were found already very early in psychedelic science, with Aghajanian (1980) reporting that while LSD and mescaline both decrease locus coeruleus activity, they make them more receptive to peripheral input. A vast range of modern studies have found increasingly global broadcasting to be a hallmark effect of psychedelics across the cortex. While psychedelics often lead to decreased within-network functional connectivity (FC; Carhart-Harris et al., 2012; Mason et al., 2021; Palhano-Fontes et al., 2015; Tagliazucchi et al., 2016; Timmermann et al., 2023), there is a marked and broad increase in between-network FC (e.g., Barrett et al., 2020; Carhart-Harris et al., 2016; Luppi et al., 2021; Madsen et al., 2021; Mason et al., 2021; Müller et al., 2018; Roseman et al., 2014; Tagliazucchi et al., 2016). These increases in functional connectivity have been found to be especially pronounced in associative regions of the cortex (Linguiti et al., 2023; Roseman et al., 2014; Tagliazucchi et al., 2016). Most existing   acute resting-state psychedelic fMRI studies have found that psychedelics lead to generally increased global brain connectivity (Erritzoe et al., 2024; Linguiti et al., 2023). Psilocybin has been found to lead to the brain spending more time in more globally coherent and integrated states (opposed to a more functionally segregated state; Lord et al., 2019). Relatedly, psychedelics have been found to decrease modularity (functional segregation) in fMRI BOLD data (Girn et al., 2022) with such changes also seeming to last beyond the acute effects (Daws et al., 2022). Moreover, psychedelics have been found to also increase the dynamical variability and repertoire of occupied brain states (Lord et al., 2019; Tagliazucchi et al., 2014). A recent study also found that the effect of using global signal regression is especially high for LSD data as compared to d-amphetamine, MDMA or a placebo (Avram et al., 2025). They also controlled for heart rate, blood pressure, temperature and head motion which did not affect the results, suggesting that the increase of global signal is not only from non-neuronal sources indicating that LSD makes neural activity more global. We suggest that the increased global cortical brain activity due to psychedelic administration is underlined to a considerable degree by their effects on the higher-order thalamus and apical dendrites, facilitating an increased distribution of cortical information. 


The acute and long-term effects of psychedelics have also been found to relate to disruptions in default mode network activity (Carhart-Harris, Muthukumaraswamy, et al., 2016; Lebedev et al., 2015; Müller et al., 2018; Roseman et al., 2014; Siegel et al., 2024; Tagliazucchi et al., 2016; for a review see Gattuso et al., 2023) which is commonly linked with self-related processing and internal mentation. Research suggests one of its functions to be contextualizing sensory processing, especially for vision (Griffis et al., 2017; R.-S. Huang & Sereno, 2013). Similar disruptions have also been reported for many other large-scale networks such dorsal attention, executive function, salience, visual and somatomotor networks (Carhart-Harris, Muthukumaraswamy, et al., 2016; Müller et al., 2018; Stoliker et al., 2024; for a review see Erritzoe et al., 2024), however there is some variability across studies (e.g., Mason et al., 2021; Stoliker et al., 2024). Relatedly, hypercontextualisation is in alignment with the predominant localization of 5-HT2A receptors to both associative and visual regions (Beliveau et al., 2017). The activity of associative regions is crucially dependent on integrating input from multiple other regions (Luppi et al., 2024). And the visual cortex stands out among unimodal regions as the processing of visual representations is especially reliant on contextual input from its surroundings (e.g., Albright & Stoner, 2002). While the precise mechanisms behind the activity of associative and visual regions are likely to have considerable idiosyncrasies, 5-HT2A receptor-dependent contextual modulation could indicate an invariant property.


Recent research utilizing intracranial microelectrode arrays in freely behaving mice has found that psychedelic compounds (i.e., LSD and ketamine) lead to high-frequency oscillations (HFOs) in several cortical and subcortical regions (Brys et al., 2023). HFOs are high-frequency activity (> 100 Hz) found in local-field potentials (LFPs; Buzs áki & Silva, 2012). HFOs were also found to be highly synchronized between even spatially distant brain regions (Brys et al., 2023). This effect seems to also follow ketamine, a non-serotonergic dissociative with some psychedelic effects, administration, potentially suggesting that coherence at high frequencies could be a crucial neural correlate of psychedelic effects (Brys et al., 2023). Increased HFOs have also been found in the mouse nucleus accumbens following the administration of LSD and DOI (Goda et al., 2013) and in the rat bilateral medial prefrontal cortex and thalamus following the administration of DOI (Hansen et al., 2019). However, the increase in thalamic HFOs is suppressed when the rats are in a locomotive active state (Hansen et al., 2019). Cortical HFOs are generally strongest in layer V - the likely nexus of psychedelic activity (Buzsáki & Silva, 2012). As HFOs have been related to functional connectivity and integration of information underlying various cognitive processes (Brys et al., 2023; Engel & da Silva, 2012; Fries, 2009), they could highlight an important neural correlate of the hypercontextualising effect of psychedelics. 




2. Psychophysical and visual effects


Emerging research looking at contextual processes during psychedelic administration has found multiple results that provide direct evidence for psychedelic hypercontextualisation. A recent study found that psilocybin dose-dependently enhances the Ebbinghaus illusion, a context-dependent perceptual effect (Fig 4A; Aqil et al., 2025). They furthermore found that psilocybin reduces neural surround suppression in early visual regions. High-dose (25 mg) psilocybin has been found to impair stimulus discrimination when the stimulus is presented with a surrounding, but not without the surrounding (Fig 4B; Swanson et al., 2024). Human participants were required to evaluate the difference in contrast between two circular gratings. On some trials one of the gratings was surrounded by orthogonal or parallel gratings. No difference was found in psilocybin’s disruptive effect on distinguishing orthogonal or parallel gratings highlighting a more general mechanism. The same experimental paradigm was later further used in another study corroborating the results (Schallmo et al., 2025). This study also further conducted a visual event-related potential analysis of the EEG signal during the experiment, finding enhanced surround suppression in the N1 component after psilocybin administration (Schallmo et al., 2025) indicating contextual effects.


There is also a considerable amount of other research which, while not directly probing contextual effects, supports hypercontextualisation. For example, psilocybin does not alter visual contrast sensitivity (stimulus-based processing) but reduces coherence sensitivity (contextual processing; fig 4C; Carter et al., 2004). In the study participants had to report the direction of motion of a sinusoidal grating in a background of white noise. The threshold where participants became unable to comprehend the direction was found to be statistically similar during the acute effects of psilocybin and after administration of a placebo, suggesting unimpaired lone stimulus perception. The same study also found that psilocybin reduced coherence sensitivity. Participants viewed a set of 100 small dots where a proportion of the dots were moving uniformly left or right and the rest of the dots were moving in a random direction. Psilocybin decreased the ability of the participants to discriminate the direction of the uniformly moving subset when the ratio of coherent to randomly moving dots was varied. This early study provides evidence for psychedelic contextual effects as participants' ability to detect a single stimulus within a non-specific noisy background was not altered. However, performance on a task based on the relative motion of several stimuli was impaired. These results also suggest that psychedelics preferentially impair global motion perception instead of local (Carter et al., 2004).


While many of the more subtle, implicit, abstract or multi-modal effects of psychedelics are difficult to assess or measure with a direct empirical approach, visual alterations offer a scientifically more accessible phenomenological shift. Furthermore, as visual alterations are often the first to arise after psychedelic administration, they have been suggested to provide a hierarchical first-step/foundation for understanding psychedelic effects (Aqil & Roseman, 2022). Psychedelic-induced changes in visual processing are often grouped to open- (OEVs) and closed-eye visuals. Psychedelic OEVs can be taxonomised into overlays, enhancements and transformations (Swanson, 2024). Overlays are alterations wherein patterns, textures or some visual content is superimposed on ‘normal’ perceptual content. A common example of overlays is dynamic geometrical patterns developing over or on certain objects or homogeneous regions of the perceptual field. Enhancements constitute increases in certain qualities of perception such as detail of textures, clarity of object borders and intensity of colour. Transformations include changes in size, shape and colour of objects, their relative perceived distance, as well as semantic visual content during higher doses (i.e., objects dynamically morphing into other objects). Overlays and transformations can be conceptualized as an increased effect of other visual features (such as specific patterns, objects or concepts) on the consciously experienced representation. Enhancements can be conceptualized as enhanced contextual processing at low levels of visual processing increasing contrast effects. A more in-depth description of how psychedelic open-eye visuals can be generally described by contextual modulation can be found in (Swanson, 2024). 


Psychedelics seem to impair processing of dynamic or moving stimuli to higher degree than static stimuli (Carter et al., 2004; Schallmo et al., 2025; Swanson et al., 2024; Vejmola et al., 2025), highlighting the importance of associations between stimuli, as opposed to specific stimulus representations. However, this does not necessarily imply that static stimuli are completely unaffected by psychedelics. The visual processing impairments found in many psychological or psychophysical psychedelic experiments are not always directly indicative of altered, hallucinatory or illusory perception. The impairments can also be caused by a disrupted ability to utilize the stimulus in task-relevant higher-order processing. Psychedelics are clearly also able to alter the perception of static stimuli, especially in higher doses, with the degree of alteration also depending on the complexity of the stimuli (Family et al., 2022; Vejmola et al., 2025). The extent of visual distortions increases with the complexity of the stimulus (e.g., faces vs shapes; Vejmola et al., 2025). Moving stimuli depend on associations with a background and high complexity stimuli have more composite parts which associate to form the stimulus. Taken together, the propensity to alter perception of dynamic and high complexity static stimuli highlight the status of associations as a primary substrate of psychedelic action. This also highlights how psychedelic OEVs depend on the boundaries between objects (or parts of objects or distinct sets of features). OEVs can take various forms, such as slight wave-like motions on the edges, an unusually enduring ‘trace’ of an object, bold or colourful outlining and even significant changes in the relative size of visual stimuli (Swanson, 2024). These basic visual alterations seem to rely upon effects at the relations between the representation of the stimulus and its surrounding visual context (Swanson, 2024). 




3. Alterations in attention and mindfulness


Alterations in attention also highlight the contextual nature of the effect of psychedelics as attention fundamentally depends on the relations and boundaries of different objects (Phillips et al., 2025). Psychedelics have powerful and complex effects on attention (Bălăeţ, 2022; Kase et al., forthcoming). While moderate and high doses psychedelics have been found to generally impair attention in various experimental paradigms (Bălăeţ, 2022; Basedow et al., 2024; Kase et al., forthcoming), there are some findings of enhanced attention primarily during microdosing (Hutten et al., 2020; Yousefi et al., 2025) but also following higher doses (Bouso et al., 2013). To further blur the picture, subjective reports of phenomenological effects suggest a more variable and complicated effect on attention between individuals, specific dosing sessions or even during different phases of a single experience (Preller & Vollenweider, 2018; Shanon, 2002). The apparent conflict between experimental decreased attention and subjectively reported enhanced attention is, at least to some degree, linked with psychedelic-induced disability to interact with external stimuli. Preliminary evidence has found that psychedelics impair speed-based measures of attention, but not accuracy (Basedow et al., 2024; Yousefi et al., 2025), supporting that the impairments are more likely to be due to task-engagement issues. Moreover, subjective reports suggest the possibility that psychedelic experiences (e.g., more positively-valenced experiences or after relinquishing the will to control) commonly feature increases in attention towards the internal flow of imagination (i.e., being immersed in the experience; Cohen, 1965; Shanon, 2002). 


There are also reports of increased mindfulness, metacognitive awareness (Amada et al., 2020; Singer et al., 2024) and broadening of attention during acute (as well as sub- and post-acute; Azmoodeh et al., 2023; Cohen, 1965; Masters & Houston, 1966; Radakovic et al., 2022) psychedelic administration which all suggest a degree or type of enhanced attention. Moreover, considerable reports mention broadening of the scope of attention or stable and clear attention to specific objects or visions and complete immersion within aspects of the psychedelic experience (Cohen, 1965; Day & Schmetkamp, 2022; Masters & Houston, 1966). This enhancement of internal attention is well illustrated by a woman participating in Sydney Cohen’s early studies (Cohen, 1965): “It [LSD] seemed to induce an effortless, absorbed and timeless power of inward turned concentration.” But such improved attention can also be directed towards salient external stimuli as highlighted here (Cohen, 1965): “Each object that I looked at held my fascinated attention […] I felt I had never really seen this garden before. I was enchanted with each plant, leaf, flower, tree trunk and the earth itself. Each blade of grass stood tip separate and distinct, edged with light. Each was supremely important.” Such patterns of altered attention suggest increased relational activity between the outlined target of attention and peripheral stimuli. Changes in mindfulness, metacognitive awareness and decentring can also be conceptualized as increased ability to relate the target of attention to higher-order values (i.e., long-term goals or a larger perspective) or alternative mindsets (e.g., Hayes et al., 2011; Jankowski & Holas, 2014; Lange, 2025). However, common reports of imagined objects rapidly shifting during intense and confusing episodes (Kaup et al., 2023; Masters & Houston, 1966) highlight the potential of psychedelics to also impair the ability to select and maintain focus on an internally created stimulus. This could in part be from hypercontextualising effects upon internal representations being able to exchange information more freely. 


4. Altered cognition and metacognition


Psychedelic alterations in cognition are also broadly in line with hypercontextualisation. Psychedelics lead to more bizarre, unrestrained cognition with increased metaphorical, symbolic and analogical thinking (Kraehenmann, Pokorny, Aicher, et al., 2017; Schmid et al., 2015; Wießner, Falchi, Maia, et al., 2022; Wießner, Falchi, Palhano-Fontes, et al., 2022). Psychedelics also lead to increases in semantic network entropy, semantic spread and support making more remote associations (Family et al., 2016). Psychedelics also increase associative learning in animals (for an unpublished review, see Caulfield et al., 2025). While under the effects of LSD, participants are more likely to make substitution errors for semantically similar items (Family et al., 2016). Such an error pattern could imply enhanced “spread of semantic network activation, such that same category items are more activated and therefore more prone to be produced as errors” and therefore increased activation of associations of a representation (Family et al., 2016). Earlier research has also highlighted that psychedelics lead to increased indirect semantic priming, highlighting that psychedelics might activate more distant associations and activate concepts usually unable to access consciousness (Spitzer et al., 1996). Similarly, low-dose LSD leads to a more discontinuous stream of thought (Wießner, Falchi, Palhano-Fontes, et al., 2022) and ‘pattern breaks’ (e.g., increased novelty, semantic distance and originality) within creative tasks (Wießner, Falchi, Maia, et al., 2022). Similarly, speech during psychedelic states has been found to become more disorganized and ‘entropic’ (Sanz et al., 2021; however see also Wießner et al., 2023). LSD has also been found to increase free association flow specifically for abstract words (Wießner, Falchi, Palhano-Fontes, et al., 2022). Psychedelics have also been found to increase primary process thinking - automatic, implicit and associative mode of thinking linked to cognition of earlier evolutionary organisms (Kraehenmann, Pokorny, Aicher, et al., 2017). Relatedly, psychedelic experiences often exhibit dream-like phenomenology - visions of far-away lands or distinct events, often with bizarre and unusual content (Kraehenmann, Pokorny, Vollenweider, et al., 2017; Sanz et al., 2018; Schmid et al., 2015; Studerus et al., 2011). All in all, the effects of psychedelics on cognition can be neatly encapsulated as increased relational activity between internal representations.


Psychedelics also commonly increase suggestibility, making contents of consciousness more likely to be modulated by other inputs (e.g., external information; Carhart-Harris et al., 2015). While this might seem to suggest increased external information, it is likely that such increased suggestibility is rather the increase of internally relevant processing based on external modulation. This is highlighted by LSD increasing social adaptation towards views that are similar to one’s own, but not towards dissimilar views (Duerler et al., 2020; McGovern et al., 2022; Preller, Schilbach, et al., 2019). Neural activity has also been found to be enhanced towards familiar stimuli and decreased towards novel stimuli (Timmermann et al., 2018; see also Horrocks et al., 2025). That is, psychedelic suggestibility seems to not enhance all external input. Rather it amplifies incoming information which is comprehensible and relevant upon integration within prior knowledge structures. Put more simply, psychedelics enhance the impression made by sensory information, but not sensory information itself and this is supported by the relative strengthening of apical activity versus basal. 


Psychedelics also commonly lead to altered metacognitive functioning such as alterations in meaning (Hartogsohn, 2018; Preller et al., 2017; Wolff et al., 2019; see also Griffiths et al., 2006, 2008), mindfulness, metacognitive awareness and decentring (Singer et al, 2024; Amada et al, 2020), various forms of insight (Davis, Barrett, So, et al., 2021; Seragnoli et al., 2025; Singer et al., 2024; Tulver et al., 2023), belief and personality changes (McGovern et al., 2022; Nayak et al., 2023; Nayak & Griffiths, 2022; Timmermann et al., 2021) or philosophical and religious contemplation (Brouwer et al., 2025; see also Timmermann et al., 2021). LSD has been found to increase the attribution of meaning to neutral or otherwise meaningless cues and this effect is largely dependent on 5-HT2A receptors (Preller et al., 2017) highlighting the role of 5-HT2A receptors in the relational associativity between internal representations.


There are also reports of increased sense of meaning during psychedelic states (Wolff et al., 2019) and people commonly rate their psychedelic sessions as some of the most meaningful, insightful and spiritually significant experiences of their lives (Griffiths et al., 2006, 2008; Johnson et al., 2017). Experiences of insight are common upon recreational, naturalistic or therapeutic use of psychedelics (Letheby, 2021; Tulver et al., 2023). Psychedelic sessions, especially if they induce mystical experiences, often exhibit a noetic quality - a sense of knowing or new understanding - not restrained to merely verbal cognition but also encompassing other aspects of phenomenology (Belser et al., 2017; Garcia-Romeu et al., 2015; Katz et al., 1968; MacLean et al., 2012; Tulver et al., 2023). A subset of such changes also includes emotional breakthrough, where people can face and resolve challenging emotions and memories (Roseman et al., 2019). Increased philosophical thinking (Brouwer et al., 2025) and subsequent changes in metaphysical and ontological beliefs (Nayak et al., 2023; Nayak & Griffiths, 2022; Timmermann et al., 2021) could also indicate amplified higher-order or abstract cognition during psychedelic sessions. Psychedelic-increased meaningfulness could be brought about by enhanced contextual influences on consciously perceived stimuli. These aspects of increased sense of meaning also provide support for a more broader contextualising effects, and can support insight and belief changes by increased associative processing and considering a broader range of perspectives (Tulver et al., 2025).


5. Creativity and cognitive flexibility


While psychedelics commonly increase subjectively-assessed creativity, objective paradigms of creativity rarely mirror these results (Baggott, 2015; Kiraga et al., 2021; Mason et al., 2021; Murphy et al., 2025; Suay et al., 2025; but do see Frecska et al., 2012; Kuypers et al., 2016; see also the alterations on cognition in subsection IV.D). There seem to be slight trends towards potential enhanced creativity post-acutely or following acute microdosing, but primarily decreased creativity acutely (for a recent review, see Bonnieux et al., 2023). There are also common occasions where creative people report benefit in their endeavours from psychedelic substances (Harman et al., 1966; Lea et al., 2020; for a review see Gandy et al., 2022) and population studies have reported positive links between creativity and psychedelic use (Rominger & Subow, 2025; Sweat et al., 2016). These conflicts can be somewhat alleviated by hypercontextualisation. On the one hand, psychedelics decrease the effect of some external sensory information (e.g., Michaiel et al., 2019; Stoliker et al., 2025) via basal desensitization, altering the perception and saliency of task-related information and impairing task-engagement. On the other hand, the random and non-specific increase in associative processing (dependent on 5-HT2A receptor binding within the brain) is likely to lead to perceptions and representations that are novel to the individual (see also Suay et al., 2025). This increase is likely to also be modulated by habitual patterns of thought (i.e., creativity is more likely to be directed towards contents of cognition more common for the individual) and personal relevance and salience (i.e., external information can support creativity, but only if it is relevant and meaningful for the individual; see also Harman et al., 1966). 


A similarly complex pattern can be seen with regards to how psychedelics affect cognitive flexibility. Animal studies have found conflicting results acutely with 25-CN-NBOH leading to impairment, DOI not having significant effects (Amodeo et al., 2020)  and psilocybin increasing cognitive flexibility (Torrado Pacheco et al., 2023), however these conflicts could reflect dose-dependent effects as the pattern of effect at a uniform dose of 1 mg/kg for all substances mimics their relative selectivity and potency at 5-HT2A receptors. However, psychedelics commonly increase cognitive flexibility in animals in the long-term (Brouns et al., 2025; Conn et al., 2024; Šabanović et al., 2024). The studies in humans are also conflicting (Kase et al., forthcoming), with classical tasks finding decreased cognitive flexibility acutely (Hutten et al., 2020; Pokorny et al., 2020; but see Bouso et al., 2013), but more complex tasks indicating also potentially enhanced aspects (Kanen et al., 2023). Post-acute findings in humans seem to align with the animal studies reporting increased cognitive flexibility (Doss et al., 2021; Murphy-Beiner & Soar, 2020). However it is likely that these enduring changes exhibit a non-linear pattern of dose-dependency, as a low-moderate dose of LSD (50 ug) has been found to impair cognitive flexibility sub-acutely  (Wießner, Olivieri, et al., 2022). Hypercontextualisation would suggest that psychedelics increase cognitive flexibility acutely in terms of making the dynamics of cognition more variable, however due to the random activation of representations and increased decoupling from external input this stream could be difficult to guide. This helps explain the positive results of cognitive flexibility in both humans and mice, whereby the flexibility is measured by more immersive and behavioural protocols (Kanen et al., 2023; Torrado Pacheco et al., 2023) but not in classical tasks (such as the Wisconsin Card Sorting Task and intra/extra-dimensional shift task; Pokorny et al., 2020; Wießner, Olivieri, Falchi, et al., 2022) that require considerable self-regulation. For example, the only study utilizing a more immersive protocol in humans found that a moderate 75 μg dose of LSD increased learning rate in a probabilistic reversal task as well as decreased tendency to repeat previous choices (Kanen et al., 2023). Furthermore, a brief period of intense, affectively ambivalent and random variability in the dynamics of cognition could lead to an enduring increase in cognitive flexibility as is suggested by the post-acute increases following larger doses (Doss et al., 2021; Murphy-Beiner & Soar, 2020; see also Brouns et al., 2025; Conn et al., 2024; Šabanović et al., 2024). As the mind settles and the ability to interact with the environment is reset, this could leave the participant in a more flexible state that can now be utilized in a task-oriented or therapeutically relevant way. This could partially be reflected by increases in trait openness following peak psychedelic experiences (Erritzoe et al., 2018; Lebedev et al., 2016; MacLean et al., 2011; see also Nour et al., 2017).


Several psychedelic compounds, such as DOI, TCB-2, psilocin and DMT have been shown to acutely enhance fear extinction learning in mice (Catlow et al., 2013; De La Fuente Revenga et al., 2021; G. Zhang et al., 2013) and rats (Pędzich et al., 2022; for a thorough review on animal studies with psychedelics see Odland et al., 2022). Fear extinction can be conceptualized as contextualising the fear-inducing stimulus against the novel non-aversive context of where the conditioned stimulus is presented (Maren et al., 2013). Psychedelic fear extinction has been found to be abolished in 5-HT2A receptor knockout mice (De La Fuente Revenga et al., 2021). 5-HT2A receptor antagonism has also been found to decrease the fear extinction effect of 3,4-methylenedioxymethamphetamine (MDMA; Young et al., 2017). 5-HT2A receptors in the medial prefrontal cortex have also been found to mediate the retrieval of contextual information in the perirhinal cortex (Morici et al., 2022). Importantly, hypercontextualisation also helps explain why the therapeutic effects of psychedelics can be long-lasting even following a single administration. While generally fear extinction learning is highly context specific (Pacheco-Estefan et al., 2025), increased contextual processing supports making the therapeutic effect from representation change more generalized and applicable in relation to a larger set of other ‘mental contents’ making the beneficial change more likely to be invoked. This is highlighted by decreased fear renewal in a novel environmental context in mice following psilocybin treatment (Woodburn et al., 2024).


6. Connectedness and disintegration of representations


One of the more commonly highlighted effects of psychedelics during higher doses, is an increased sense of connectedness which can linger even beyond the acute effects (Amada et al., 2020; Carhart-Harris, Erritzoe, et al., 2018; Forstmann & Sagioglou, 2025; Kałużna et al., 2022; Watts et al., 2017, 2022). While studies have examined connectedness to nature and other people, evidence also suggests enhanced “sense of connectedness with one’s own senses, body, and emotions”, alongside transpersonal bonds, purpose, meaning, and spiritual values following both naturalistic and therapeutic administration (Watts et al., 2022). This broad and highly general set of aspects, which become more connected during psychedelic use, could be summarized as ‘increased connectedness between mental representations’.


Psychedelics also lead to alterations in the sense of self, ranging from decreases to narrative and autobiographical aspects of self, bodily and mental agency; disintegration of a bodily felt sense of self; up until a complete loss of subject-object duality and all felt selfhood (Letheby, 2021; Milliere, 2020; Nour et al., 2016). We suggest that these can be considered as increased connectivity between these self-related representations to other representations, which leads to a complete disintegration of the self-model during the peak (see also Deane, 2020; Laukkonen & Slagter, 2021). A partial effect would only alter some aspects of the sense of self (e.g., autobiographical sense of self, some parts of the bodily sense of self) based mainly on regional pharmacokinetic differences. An even higher dose can lead to experiences of minimal phenomenal content, whereby contents of consciousness are disrupted (Preller & Vollenweider, 2018). This could stem from overloading of contextual information. A perceptually activated representation could be affected by contextualising input from so many other representations that it ceases to have an informative frame of reference and is disrupted. An equivalent description would be that hyperactive relational communication breaks down boundaries of specific representations at many levels of hierarchical complexity simultaneously, leading to their disintegration.


This general pattern of increased relational activity between representations is also likely to alter the perception of time and space, as is common during psychedelic experiences (MacLean et al., 2012; Preller & Vollenweider, 2018; Studerus et al., 2010, 2011; Wackermann et al., 2008; Wittmann et al., 2007). These alterations may stem from heightened contextual modulation, where enhanced interactions between neural representations disrupt the usual binding of spatial and temporal features, leading to phenomena like time dilation or cessation of its flow and spatial dissolution (see Deane, 2020). Another common psychedelic effect, which modulates binding, is synesthesia - the mixing of different sensory modalities, whereby information from one sense affects or leads to a perceptual experience in another sensory modality (Sinke et al., 2012; Terhune et al., 2016). Synesthesia could be enacted by increased contextual information flow from regions responsible for processing other modalities to the cells instantiating the active content of consciousness.


7. Psychedelic neuroplasticity


One of the most discussed effects of psychedelics is their ability to increase neuroplasticity (for reviews see Agnorelli et al., 2025; De Vos et al., 2021; Inserra, De Gregorio, & Gobbi, 2021). A range of preclinical studies have suggested that various psychedelic compounds lead to increases in markers of neuroplasticity as well as neuronal and dendritic growth (Jefsen et al., 2021; Lima da Cruz et al., 2018; Ly et al., 2018; Moliner et al., 2023; Nardou et al., 2023; Raval et al., 2021; Shao et al., 2021; Vargas et al., 2023). Preliminary research in humans has also found psychedelic-induced increases in neuroplasticity markers (Almeida et al., 2019; Holze, Vizeli, et al., 2021; Hutten et al., 2021; but see Galvão-Coelho et al., 2020), however direct measurements in humans are yet to be published (see Lyons et al., 2024 for first unpublished results). While neuroplasticity does not constitute direct support for hypercontextual effects, it offers secondary evidence as the changes in neural representation can rely upon altered relational activity between representations. Several studies have suggested that 5-HT2A receptor agonism has an important role in psychedelic neuroplasticity (Ly et al., 2018; Shao et al., 2021, 2025; Vargas et al., 2023), however, other receptors such as sigma-1 and TrkB have also been touted as potential targets (Inserra, 2018; Moliner et al., 2023 however see also Jain et al., 2025). It is likely that psychedelic neuroplasticity is primarily synaptic and dendritic in nature (Calder & Hasler, 2023; see also Dong et al., 2024; Ly et al., 2018, 2021) potentially centrally affecting apical dendrites. Furthermore, as psychedelics are most likely to affect psychological processes (as opposed to visceral, autonomic or motor functions), it is parsimonious to assume that psychedelic neuroplasticity is also, to some degree, limited to neural substrata relevant for cognitive and psychological processes. 


8. Hypercontextual 5-HT2A receptors




Several lines of research on 5-HT2A receptors provide further evidence on their contextual function.  Several serotonin 5-HT2A receptor agonists have been found to increase associative learning (Harvey, 2003; Harvey et al., 1988; see also Caulfield et al., 2025) in rabbits and rodents. Some 5-HT2A receptor antagonists, such as MDL-11,939, ritanserin and mianserin disrupt associative learning (Harvey, 2003). 5-HT2A receptor knockout mice show impairment in associative learning, but similar performance to wild-type mice on single item or location recognition tasks (Barre et al., 2016). Increased 5-HT2A receptor density enhances associative learning in rabbits (Harvey et al., 2004). Serotonin depletion has also been linked with decreased associative learning in primates (Clarke et al., 2005). Conditioned response acquisition studies in animals suggest that psychedelics enhance conditioned response learning while not affecting responses to unpaired stimuli (Harvey, 2003). 


5-HT2A receptor agonism also mediates cross-modal plasticity  (i.e., neural restructuring of sensory regions following loss of one type of sensory input corresponding to increased functionality in the intact sensory modality) of cortical circuits. Reorganization of the visual cortex following monocular enucleation (i.e., surgical eye removal) in mice (Lombaert et al., 2018) and the barrel cortex in rats (Jitsuki et al., 2011) have been shown to be blocked by ketanserin. Studies have found that development of transmodal and associative brain regions depends on 5-HT2A receptors (Xing et al., 2020; see also Mitazaki et al., 2020; Sood et al., 2018).  These studies support that 5-HT2A receptors are involved in functionally linking various brain regions together.


5-HT2A receptors also seem to affect memory and hippocampal processing, both of which crucially rely on contextualisation. For example, 5-HT2A receptors have also been found to affect hippocampal novelty processing and thus, memory (Schott et al., 2011). 5-HT2A receptors have been found to play a role specifically in episodic memory (De Quervain et al., 2003; Wagner et al., 2008). Highlighting the role of 5-HT2A receptors in contextual memory processing directly, the blockade of mPFC 5-HT2A receptors has been found to impair contextual memory but not single-item recognition memory (Bekinschtein et al., 2013). 5-HT2A receptors in the medial prefrontal cortex have also been found to mediate the retrieval of contextual information in the perirhinal cortex (Morici et al., 2022). Several studies have found hippocampal activity and functional connectivity to be altered by psychedelic administration or even related to the subjective effects of psychedelics. For example, psychedelics have been found to alter hippocampal and parahippocampal connectivity (Carhart-Harris et al., 2017; Kaelen et al., 2016; Kometer et al., 2015; Lebedev et al., 2015; Mason et al., 2020) and several studies have found these alterations to correlate with subjective effects of psychedelics (Kometer et al., 2015; Lebedev et al., 2015; Mason et al., 2020). More specifically, psychedelic-induced spiritual experiences and insightfulness has been found to relate to increased synchronization between the retrosplenial cortex, parahippocampus and lateral orbitofrontal cortex (Kometer et al., 2015). Parahippocampal regions are commonly related to memory encoding and retrieval, but crucially have also been implicated in associative context processing (Aminoff et al., 2013; Bar, 2004; Bar et al., 2008; Rudy, 2009). Similarly, hippocampal regions are often linked with memory-related processes and spatial navigation, but also with contextual processing (Maurer & Nadel, 2021; Phillips & LeDoux, 1994; Rudy, 2009; Sakon & Kahana, 2022). 




V. Discussion


The following section will highlight various implications, predictions, key future questions and limitations of apical hypercontextualisation.


1. Relationships with existing theories


Apical hypercontextualisation emphasizes enhanced contextual modulation through 5-HT2A receptor agonism and effect on thalamocortical loops. Here, we will briefly address how apical hypercontextualisation relates to existing theories of psychedelic action. Apical hypercontextualisation stands out by being centrally grounded on neuroanatomical and electrophysiological findings of psychedelic substances, while other existing theories generally emphasize circuit effects (Carhart-Harris & Friston, 2019; Doss et al., 2022; Vollenweider & Smallridge, 2022) or theoretical mechanisms such as alterations in predictive processing (Carhart-Harris & Friston, 2019; Corlett et al., 2019; Safron et al., 2025) or information-theoretic measures (Carhart-Harris, 2018; Carhart-Harris et al., 2014). Yet there are considerable dimensions of overlap with previous ideas. Apical hypercontextualisation roughly agrees with RElaxed/ALtered Beliefs Under pSychedelics (REBUS/ALBUS), that changes in beliefs (or more generally mental representations) have to arise from some kind of relaxation (Carhart-Harris & Friston, 2019; Safron et al., 2025). However, we suggest it is primarily a relaxation or alteration of contextual factors instead of higher-order representations. In this sense, apical hypercontextualisation is more in line with ALBUS regarding possible bidirectional alteration (both strengthening and weakening) of priors (Safron et al., 2025). Instead of suggesting a dose-dependence on altering the strength of priors, we suggest that the dose-dependence is related to the hierarchical complexity of altered mental representations. REBUS also highlights the importance of certain brain regions which are especially relevant for contextual processing such as regions of the default mode network and hippocampal/parahippocampal areas (Carhart-Harris & Friston, 2019). We agree that psychedelics could have more pronounced effects on these regions. This could be a consequence of enhanced apical thalamocortical loop activity depending on the regional variability of the 5-HT2A receptor distribution. Furthermore, similarly to the entropic brain hypothesis, we also emphasize increased variability in brain activity linked to alterations in mental representations and hierarchical complexity of mental representations (Carhart-Harris, 2018) arising through increased associative processing at apical dendrites (see M. Larkum, 2013). While our proposal has some similarities to the cortica-striato-thalamo-cortical (CSTC) model, we focus on the biological cellular level, emphasizing the role of various dendritic compartments with the main mechanistic onus on altered internal and contextual processing rather than detailed cortical-subcortical network connectivity and increasing bottom-up information (Vollenweider & Smallridge, 2022). The cellular mechanisms discussed here could be integrated with circuit-level effects (such as R. L. Carhart-Harris & Friston, 2019; Doss et al., 2022; Vollenweider & Smallridge, 2022) or provide a physical substrate for the computations explicated in existing theories (Carhart-Harris & Friston, 2019; Corlett et al., 2019).


Current discourse on the effects of psychedelics almost invariantly describes the effects of psychedelics as altering top-down and/or bottom-up processing (Carhart-Harris & Friston, 2019; Corlett et al., 2019; Vollenweider & Smallridge, 2022). While it is likely that psychedelics do indeed affect this dimension of neural processing, it is not clear whether the effects are best captured by a distinct mechanism within this paradigm. Studies have found results of both increased (Alamia et al., 2020; Alonso et al., 2015; Timmermann et al., 2018) or decreased bottom-up flow (Horrocks et al., 2025; Michaiel et al., 2019; see also Heiss et al., 1973; Moore et al., 1976). Recent results with 5-MeO-DMT have also found decreases in both bottom-up and top-down flow across the cortex (Blackburne et al., 2025). Studies also suggest that psychedelics have various effects on pre-pulse inhibition (a top-down effect) in humans, with DMT found to have no effect, LSD showing decreases in PPI and psilocybin showing bidirectional effects dependent on inter-stimulus interval (for a review see Halberstadt & Geyer, 2016). The inadequacy of the top-down/bottom-up paradigm in explaining psychedelic effects is also supported by highly conflicting theoretical views and empirical evidence. Even prominent theories have posited polar opposite effects within the paradigm. REBUS suggests a relaxation of high-order priors (Carhart-Harris & Friston, 2019), strong prior model suggests a strengthening of priors (Corlett et al., 2019), ALBUS suggests dose-dependent effects on priors (Safron et al., 2025) and CSTC suggests increased bottom-up flow (Vollenweider & Smallridge, 2022). Moreover, some views still largely rely on the idea that the thalamus mainly embodies bottom-up drive (Vollenweider & Smallridge, 2022), however the thalamus also has important modulatory and controlling influence over cortical activity mainly via higher-order nuclei (Roth et al., 2016; Sherman, 2016; Shine et al., 2023). Psychedelics seem to affect various thalamic regions, with higher doses and concurrent higher intensity experiences seemingly related to the connectivity of higher order hubs such as the mediodorsal thalamus (Avram et al., 2022; Inserra, De Gregorio, Rezai, et al., 2021; Siegel et al., 2024; for comparison see Delli Pizzi et al., 2023). Hypercontextualisation suggests increased effect of internal (vs sensory) information and a more complex effect on priors of various hierarchical order (potentially dependent on intrinsic excitatory activity). This aligns better with psychedelic phenomenology as psychedelics affect priors in a hierarchically dose-dependent way (e.g., low-order priors accounting for object boundaries and colours are affected before high-order priors such as aspects of sense of self or complex concepts). 


2. Bidirectional electrophysiological effects of psychedelics


It is currently unclear whether the acute effect of psychedelics is dependent on excitatory effects within some subset of neurons or even some subset of layer V pyramidal neurons or more complex effects. The electrophysiological effects of psychedelics are diverse not only due to their broad receptor binding profiles (Jain et al., 2025; Ray, 2010; Rickli et al., 2016) but also because their direct 5-HT2A-dependent effect is not as clearly excitatory as it is for serotonin (Aghajanian & Marek, 1997, 1999b; González-Maeso et al., 2007; Kossatz et al., 2024; Lambe & Aghajanian, 2006a; Liu et al., 2022; Rodriguiz et al., 2021). 


While the paradigmatic view paints psychedelics as excitatory agents (e.g., Nichols, 2016; van Elk & Yaden, 2022; Vollenweider & Smallridge, 2022), a host of studies, both old and emerging, indicate that the total additive effects of psychedelics lead to mainly decreased brain activity (reviewed in Hidalgo Jiménez et al., 2025). Already some of the earliest studies looking at the effect of psychedelics on neurons in the dorsal raphe - the serotonergic centre of the brain - found predominantly inhibitory effects (although likely to be 5-HT1A-mediated; Aghajanian & Lakoski, 1984; Vandermaelen & Aghajanian, 1983). Later research has shown such effects to be considerably more prevalent across the brain. Several fMRI (Carhart-Harris et al., 2012; Carhart-Harris, Muthukumaraswamy, et al., 2016), EEG (Timmermann et al., 2019, 2023) and MEG (Carhart-Harris, Muthukumaraswamy, et al., 2016; Muthukumaraswamy et al., 2013) studies of acute psychedelic administration in humans find an almost exclusively inhibitory effect. Moreover, studies in rodents using fMRI (Ghaw et al., 2024), intracortical electrodes (Brys et al., 2023; Dearnley et al., 2023; Riga et al., 2014; Wood et al., 2012) or in vitro assessments of neural firing (Ekins et al., 2023; Wang et al., 2025) also suggest mainly reduced activity. Changes in large-scale brain dynamics (including broad reductions) seem to be dependent on the 5-HT2A receptor, as its antagonists block the effect (Celada et al., 2008; Preller et al., 2018; Puig, 2003). There is also some indication that inhibitory effects could be to some degree also 5-HT2A dependent as they are reversed by selective 5-HT2A antagonist volinanserin (M100907; Puig, 2003; Tian et al., 2016) and can be induced by strongly 5-HT2A selective psychedelics (with negligible 5-HT1A affinity, e.g., 25CN-NBOH; Ekins et al., 2023; Wang et al., 2025). 


However, several other mechanisms could have important roles in inhibitory effects, which have not been fully dismissed. Activity at other receptors (most notably 5-HT1A or group II metabotropic glutamate receptors (mGluR2); Lambe & Aghajanian, 2006b; Martin & Nichols, 2016) or recurrent and direct 5-HT2A-dependent interneuron activation could also have important inhibitory effects on network-level activity (Carhart-Harris et al., 2012; Muir et al., 2024). Layer V pyramidal cells also exhibit strong recurrent connectivity between nearby interneurons, making it a likely crucial inhibitory mechanism (Bodor et al., 2025). Then again, there are also neurons which respond to psychedelics with increased dendritic excitability and firing (Martin & Nichols, 2016; Muir et al., 2024; Schmitz et al., 2025; Shao et al., 2025). There is a swathe of early studies suggesting that psychedelic 5-HT2A agonism has a slight depolarizing effect on somatic membrane potential (e.g., Araneda & Andrade, 1991; Tanaka & North, 1993; for a review, see Marek & Schoepp, 2021) and biochemical markers of excitatory neural activity (e.g., extracellular glutamate levels; Muschamp et al., 2004; Scruggs et al., 2003; and fos expression; Martin & Nichols, 2016; Zhai et al., 2003) are generally increased by psychedelics.


Such complex bidirectionality of the effects suggests that the effect of psychedelics could be better explained by mechanisms at other levels of scale. Herein, we propose that focusing on sub-neuronal processes - specifically, the apical dendrites - provides an optimal explanatory substrate. We maintain that excitation of extratelencephalic layer V neurons is likely essential for psychedelic action, yet this is a downstream consequence of effects at the apical compartment. The variability and cell-type-specific responses also imply that psychedelics primarily modulate input without necessarily leading to consistently excitatory consequences.




3. Apical drive and oneirogen hypothesis


The effect of the apical compartment on the somatic activity of layer V neurons has also been suggested to underlie dreamful sleep (Aru, Siclari, et al., 2020). This phenomenon - termed apical drive - whereby the firing of layer V neurons, decoupled from sensory information, becomes driven by the internal, leading to bizarre and imaginative phenomenal experiences during sleep (Aru, Siclari, et al., 2020; see also Phillips et al., 2018). Another recent unpublished perspective also suggests that psychedelic action is underlined by oneirogenic effects via increased apical drive (Bredenberg et al., 2025). Here we are broadly in line with that perspective, but suggest that contextual modulation to be a more precise generalization of psychedelic effects as psychedelics increase global (Carhart-Harris, Muthukumaraswamy, et al., 2016; Luppi et al., 2021; Madsen et al., 2021; Mason et al., 2021; Müller et al., 2018; Roseman et al., 2014; Tagliazucchi et al., 2016) and high-order thalamocortical (Inserra, De Gregorio, Rezai, et al., 2021; Siegel et al., 2024) functional connectivity leading to increased associative processing and also affect primarily contextual processing in vision (Aqil et al., 2025; Swanson et al., 2024). Sleep exhibits a different pattern, primarily leading to decreased cortical connectivity (e.g., Massimini et al., 2005) distinguishing it from the psychedelic state.




4. Non-serotonergic psychedelics, cholinergic hallucinogens and non-pharmacological methods for inducing altered states 


While we currently emphasize the importance of the apical thalamocortical loop in the action of serotonergic psychedelics, it could be that non-serotonergic mind-altering substances also rely on this anatomical structure. For example, the NMDA-antagonist ketamine also increases apical calcium signaling (Ali et al., 2020) similarly to psilocybin (Shao et al., 2025). Moreover, while ketamine has a predominantly dissociative and anaesthetic effect, its partial psychedelic effects could be conceptualized as a consequence of altering the relative strength of apical signals by negatively modulating NMDA spikes primarily in tuft regions or on thalamocortical afferents (Phillips et al., 2018). Cholinergic hallucinogens, such as scopolamine, atropine and diphenhydramine, which often lead to more delirious and frightful consciousness alterations, could also touch upon processes highlighted here, as cholinergic systems also affect input into layer I apical dendrites (Aru, Siclari, et al., 2020). We tentatively suggest that their consciousness-altering effects could also be underlined by affecting input into apical dendrites, without leading to increases in global functional connectivity.


Hypercontextualisation can also help understand how various other methods, such as meditation, virtual reality based tools, sensory deprivation and flicker light stimulation, can also induce alterations of consciousness. A common characteristic of these methods is that they primarily operate by decreasing variability of sensory input patterns. In the slightly simplified model we are proposing, this is roughly analogous to the decreased basal excitability. However, a similar increased connectivity pattern at the apical compartment is not directly induced by these methods on a comparable level to substance-based methods. But these methods could serve as tools to support inducing apical hypercontextualisation by decreasing the basal effects. As external input has a weaker effect upon basal compartment activity, this could ‘leave room’ for more internal and associative processing. Prolonged meditation and advanced ability of meditation do seem to induce similar alterations in functional connectivity (see for example Bremer et al., 2022; Luders et al., 2011; Z. Zhang et al., 2021). Flicker-light-induced visual alterations also seem to depend on hyperconnectivity between higher-order thalamic and visual cortex regions (Amaya et al., 2025). The difference to psychedelic states could be that as the flicker light effects depend on visual sensory input, the connectivity of alterations tend to be constrained to the visual cortex (Amaya et al., 2025), while the pharmacological psychedelic effect has a broader effect in the brain (e.g., Siegel et al., 2024). Similar effects could be utilized by virtual reality (Kaup et al., 2023) and sensory deprivation methods (e.g., Andersen et al., 2014), which could potentially also affect brain activity related to auditory and somatic sensations.


5. Therapeutic hypercontextualisation


Hypercontextualisation suggests that the therapeutic effects of psychedelics arise from increased information exchange between mental representations, developing adaptive and non-pathological abstractions if pathological representations are contextualised in relation to broader and more adaptive representations (see also Dourron et al., 2022). Therapeutically beneficial change in mental representations can therefore take two forms: simplification (i.e., model or uncertainty reduction; extinction of pathological relations; Aragones et al., 2005; Friston et al., 2017) and connecting pathological reactions with a solution representation. Relatedly, self-reported therapeutic improvements following psychedelic use are strongly correlated with changes in identification with the pathological condition (Pouyan et al., 2025) highlighting improved and more-detailed self and ailment representations. In brief, the therapeutic effects of psychedelics are instantiated by revision of relations between representations or, even more succinctly, recontextualisation of pathological beliefs arising from hypercontextualised processing.


However, clearly not any kind of recontextualisation is beneficial. We suggest that psychedelics induce a dose-dependent degree of contextualisation which is modulated by external sensory information and also some intrinsic top-down processes (e.g., (not) ‘letting go’, trait mindfulness, retaining control over and directing mental content; see for example Griffiths et al., 2018; Smigielski et al., 2019; see also Carhart-Harris, Roseman, et al., 2018). Both external sensory information (e.g., Hansen et al., 2019; Siegel et al., 2024) and strong will to control one’s mental processing can block the contextualisation from becoming ‘global’ leading to incomplete and ‘partial’ revision due to localized contextualisation. Based on this, the central risk of psychedelics could be ‘incomplete’, ‘partial’ and ‘local’ hypercontextualisation, which might cause delusions, false insights and increased pathological symptoms (Laukkonen et al., 2020; McGovern et al., 2024). However, if contextualisation is allowed to ‘globalize’, it is more likely that mental conflicts can be relieved. More global processing allows representations to be functionally optimized in relation to a broader part of the domain of all mental representations. Furthermore, internal conflicts are often salient and people tend to ruminate on them excessively, which might make them more likely to be activated during psychedelic experiences and opening them up for recontextualisation. However, as higher doses also present an increased risk of emotionally complex and anxious states as well as other side effects (Perez et al., 2023), determining the optimal dose requires consideration of individual- and situation-specific factors in order to balance the dangers with the potential of more global recontextualisation.  Hypercontextualisation posits that there is, on average, an intrinsic therapeutic benefit to the 5-HT2A-dependent acute effects of psychedelics via global processing. Though it is important to highlight, that some therapeutic benefit could also require other receptors.


F. Predictions


The central predictions from apical hypercontextualisation are that psychedelic 5-HT2A receptor agonism is linked with alterations in contextual modulation within perception (Aqil et al., 2025; Swanson et al., 2024). Another prediction is that psychedelic-induced firing in a subset of extra-telencephalic layer V pyramidal cells is primarily driven by apical compartment activity and that the alteration of firing in those cells is the primary effect of psychedelic action, leading to downstream repercussions in thalamocortical loop and brain network activity (Schmitz et al., 2025; Shao et al., 2025). The complete acute effect of psychedelics is also dependent on thalamically-mediated glutamatergic feedback into layer I and its modulation by presynaptic 5-HT2A receptors (Barre et al., 2016). The central experimental test for apical hypercontextualisation relies on direct electrophysiological tests of how the apical and basal dendritic compartments respond to stimulation within the specific subset of ET layer V pyramidal cells which are excited by psychedelics. However, it is likely plausible that there is temporal variability of how psychedelics alter neural activity even within a specific cell (see Jain et al., 2025), potentially dependent upon binding duration, receptor internalization, pharmacodynamic desensitization and recurrent network dynamics. This temporal variability is important to address in future studies.


While there is some existing evidence suggesting that psychedelics affect contextual modulation (Aqil et al., 2025; Carter et al., 2004; Michaiel et al., 2019; Schallmo et al., 2025; Swanson et al., 2024), research utilizing other related experimental paradigms is still missing. There could be benefits in investigating the effects of psychedelics on contextual disambiguation, selective attention, Gestalt grouping, associative learning, priming effects as well as context-based illusions (Phillips et al., 2015; Swanson, 2024). While some preliminary evidence highlighting increased priming during psychedelics does exist (Family et al., 2016; Spitzer et al., 1996), larger studies are required for conclusive claims. Furthermore, the existing evidence for visual hypercontextual modulation has thus far only employed psilocybin (Aqil et al., 2025; Swanson et al., 2024) and DOI (Michaiel et al., 2019), but we predict that similar results will also be found with other serotonergic psychedelics (e.g., LSD, DMT and so forth). Hypercontextualisation also predicts that psychedelics weaken perceptual locking phenomena. Relatedly, investigating other contextual processes such as perception of ambiguous imagery and illusions under the effect of psychedelics could be beneficial. One lucrative form of research for understanding psychedelic effects could be to investigate how they alter different visual stimuli. Analyzing phenomenological reports of OEVs upon controlled visual stimuli (e.g., altering the patternedness or hierarchical complexity) could help better elucidate the various forms of visual alterations. Under the assumption that visual alterations can be utilized as a low-level ‘model’ for the more complex cognitive effects of psychedelics (Aqil & Roseman, 2022), this could be used as a useful paradigm for investigating the basic mechanisms of psychedelic phenomenology. 


A theory of psychedelic action needs to account for the dose-dependent alteration of representations with increasing hierarchical complexity (smaller doses start affecting a smaller visual resolution and simple visual objects, with higher doses affecting high visual resolution and complex multi-modal and abstract objects) and the near-complete disruption of phenomenal content at high doses. Hypercontextualisation is aligned with findings of increased global functional connectivity that correlates with intensity of phenomenological experience and hierarchical complexity of experiential alteration. In fact, global functional connectivity has been found to be very strongly correlated (r > 0.8) with subjective measures (Siegel et al., 2024). Hypercontextualisation predicts that the hierarchical complexity of experiential alteration is positively correlated with GFC and at experiential peaks the signal becomes so global that the neural representations of contents of consciousness are completely disrupted. An alternative would be that at peak states GFC plummets. To state it precisely, we suggest that GFC would still increase with dose, but the intensity of phenomenological effects peak at some threshold of GFC where the representation of contents of consciousness becomes completely disrupted. Relatedly, we also suggest that increased HFOs could be an important neural correlate for the acute psychedelic effects (e.g., Brys et al., 2023; Hansen et al., 2019).


G. Future questions


There are many questions still unanswered regarding the effects of psychedelics and relevant to the hypothesis of hypercontextualisation, which we have not addressed here. Perhaps the most obvious questions are on the specific role of various receptors, cell types and targeted effects at different regions. Psychedelics also have various non-neuronal impacts, which have been suggested to underlie some aspects of their therapeutic and consciousness-altering effects (e.g., Flanagan & Nichols, 2018; Inserra, De Gregorio, & Gobbi, 2021). 


While there is strong reason to believe that 5-HT2A receptors are crucial mediators of psychedelic effects, something more is likely to be required for their acute and therapeutic effects to arise. Possibilities include biased agonism (Hatzipantelis & Olson, 2024), concurrent 5-HT2A and 5-HT1A agonism (Savalia et al., 2021), effects at potassium M-channels (Wang et al., 2025), activity at a subset of 5-HT2A-expressing neurons (Martin & Nichols, 2016; Shao et al., 2025; Vargas et al., 2023) or 5-HT2A and mGluR heterocomplex activity modulation (Lambe & Aghajanian, 2006b; Martin & Nichols, 2016). Long-term effects have also been suggested to depend on binding at tyrosine receptor kinase B (TrkB; Moliner et al., 2023 Moliner et al, 2023; but see Jain et al., 2025), but it is likely that 5-HT2A receptors have a modulatory or even permissive role (Calder & Hasler, 2023). Investigating these mechanisms can help us falsify and test apical hypercontextualisation and elucidate the anatomical structures and functional mechanisms of psychedelic actions.


It is further unclear to what degree the effects of psychedelics are mediated by activity within the intermediate oblique dendrites, the apical tufts or the apical trunk. Effects on oblique dendrites (where 5-HT2A receptors seem to also be densely located) could instantiate intracortical contextual processing as oblique dendrites predominantly gain contextual input from layer II/III pyramidal neurons. This could also provide a substrate for the visual alterations of psychedelics (Michaiel et al., 2019). Psychedelics could also modulate output from non-specific thalamic regions (Aru et al., 2019) and input into oblique dendrites. Furthermore, the precise role of the thalamus and its constituent nuclei in affecting cortical network activity during psychedelic administration is another important aspect to address in more detail (Avram et al., 2022; Delli Pizzi et al., 2023; Inserra, De Gregorio, Rezai, et al., 2021). Relatedly, it would be important to combine these cellular and dendritic phenomena discussed herein with higher (e.g., various levels of brain networks) and smaller (e.g., molecular and genetic) orders with more mechanistic detail.


Hypercontextualisation also explicates important considerations for task-based investigations of psychedelic states. As the psychedelic experience (both its content and intensity) can be modulated by the patterns of external information routed into the basal dendrites (e.g., Hansen et al., 2019; Siegel et al., 2024), this highlights the need for experimental paradigms designed specifically for altered states (Kase et al., forthcoming). Not only might conventional experimental paradigms created for regular states of consciousness fail to capture their intended property due to disrupted external information flow and consequent impaired task-engagement (Kase et al., forthcoming), but this flow can also suppress or modulate the unfolding of psychedelic activity. Hypercontextualisation provides a theoretical grounding that emphasizes the need for several considerations upon designing psychedelic experiments. As recently highlighted (Pronovost-Morgan et al., 2025), reporting environmental factors and properties can be especially crucial in psychedelic research. To better utilize various experimental paradigms, it could be necessary to lay the groundwork by specifying how experimental paradigms and environments modulate the psychedelic experience. Research into altered states of consciousness could require adaptation of the experiments, taking into account the psychedelic-induced decoupling of perception and sensory information, increased attention on environmental factors as well as considering other idiosyncrasies of psychedelic states. Utilizing more immersive and behavioural paradigms could also help us probe these complicated states with more precision and validity.


VI. Limitations
        
The most crucial limitation of apical hypercontextualisation is the neglect of the role of cells and complex network effects. Most relevantly, other 5-HT2A receptor expressing cells, such as L2/3 cells in the visual cortex, interneurons and layer 6 cells, which could be crucially and directly modulated by psychedelics, are omitted from the current conceptualization. For example, it is likely that visual contextual processing can be more dependent on altered activity within L2/3 cells (e.g., Michaiel et al., 2019), especially within the visual cortex where contextualisation is necessary at a hierarchically more rudimentary level. However, the current proposal does not address these differences in order to provide a more accessible account to the cellular effects of psychedelics. Furthermore, a considerable subset of GABAergic cells (~ 13-30%) contain 5-HT2A receptor mRNA (De Almeida & Mengod, 2007) or express 5-HT2A receptors (Ju et al., 2020).  In cortical GABAergic cells 5-HT2A receptor mRNA is less prevalent, being found in 13-25% of cells (De Almeida & Mengod, 2007). In the mouse insular cortex, around 70% of pyramidal neurons and 30% of GABAergic neurons have been found to express 5-HT2A receptors (Ju et al., 2020). The fact that the majority of 5-HT2A receptors are localized into layer V (e.g., Chiu et al., 2023; Weber & Andrade, 2010) does suggest a primary role for these cells in psychedelic effects. Moreover, their considerable relevance for conscious processing also provides further support for their relevance in underlying alterations in consciousness (Aru et al., 2019; Aru, Suzuki, et al., 2020).


A considerable amount of the research leveraged for the theory is based on 5-HT2A agonists which are rarely used in humans (e.g., DOI and DOB), and in some cases lack rigorous human testing and proper validation of psychedelic acute effects (e.g., 25CN-NBOH, TCB-2). While animal behavioural assays provide a powerful tool (Halberstadt et al., 2020), there are some limitations for the head-twitch response which are used as a primary assay in many of the empirical studies referred here. For example, some non-psychedelic substances (such as 5-hydroxytryptophan) also induce head-twitch responses (Shahar et al., 2022) and the face validity of the test is weak (Fantegrossi et al., 2008; Odland et al., 2022). Studies utilizing animal models can guide research directions, but the final answers regarding the mechanisms of psychedelics require human administration with concurrent assessment of the subjective effects.




VII. Conclusion


We have leveraged the cellular anatomy of layer V pyramidal neurons - the major targets of classical psychedelics - to elucidate the general functional mechanisms of psychedelic action. At the core of our proposal, we highlighted important mechanisms crucial for understanding psychedelic action and grounding it biologically. The 5-HT2A-dependent effect of psychedelics targets apical thalamocortical loops through multiple mechanisms providing anatomical evidence for contextual modulation as a central and general mechanism of psychedelic action. We reviewed many research directions that support our main idea: psychedelics do not alter direct neural representations of stimuli, but rather affect relations between representations.
